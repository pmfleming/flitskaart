A Dutch conversation built around the learner’s topic

Waar wil je vandaag over praten? The learner gives a topic in Dutch or English. The app does a short lookup, chooses someone with a stake in the topic, and opens in that person’s voice. The same person holds the conversation and helps the learner express themselves in Dutch. One exchange can be enough. The learner can also follow an interesting thread for as long as they like.

What it sounds like

For “repair cafés”, the app finds that visitors can bring broken items and repair them alongside volunteers. It chooses Noor, a fictional volunteer who thinks learning to fix something yourself is worth a little inconvenience. The learner sees who Noor is and can open the source. Noor’s opinion belongs to her. Repair Café International on how a visit works

Noor · “Ik zou een kapotte koptelefoon eerst proberen te repareren. Zelfs als het niet lukt, leer je er iets van.”

Leerling · “Ik koop liever een nieuwe. Ik heb hem nodig voor mijn werk en ik heb geen tijd om te wachten.”

Noor · “Als je hem voor je werk nodig hebt, snap ik dat. Zou je de oude bewaren om hem later te repareren?”

Leerling · “Misschien. Hoe zeg je ‘spare’?”

Noor · “Als reserve. Ik zou hem als reserve houden. Heb je thuis meer spullen die je om die reden bewaart?”

Noor gives the learner something to agree or disagree with. The learner’s reason changes her suggestion. When asked for a word, she supplies it inside a sentence the learner might actually want to say. The learner could end here, tell a story about something they kept, or ask Noor why she enjoys fixing things.

How a conversation works

  1. Research the learner’s topic. Read one or two relevant sources, preferably in Dutch. Look for a dated report when recency matters and a reliable explainer for an enduring topic. Keep a few useful facts with their links and dates when available. If a lookup fails, offer a retry or a conversation based on the learner’s own experience.
  2. Give the partner a considered opinion. Choose a role, a starting view and a concrete reason for it. Think about what might qualify or change that view. For a personal topic, that opinion can be a gentle preference. Open in level-appropriate Dutch with something the learner can react to. Use sourced details accurately and attribute personal judgments to the fictional speaker.
  3. Talk and teach in the same voice. Respond to what the learner means. Share an observation, answer their question, agree on a particular point or explain why the partner sees it differently. Leave space for the learner to take the lead. Model a useful phrase when it fits, ask for clarification when meaning breaks down, and explain explicitly when asked. At A2, a short either-or question can help someone get started. At B1, the partner might pick up on an exception the learner raised. Keep replay, slower audio, translations and corrections within reach.
  4. Let the learner set the length. Make Afronden available from the opening and honour a clear goodbye in chat. Ending closes the exchange immediately. The saved transcript, optional recap and draft cards are available afterwards. During a longer conversation, follow changes of angle and remember earlier reasons, agreements and useful language. Continue until the learner chooses to finish, including after they have successfully used a new expression.

This draws on the CEFR’s emphasis on purposeful interaction and Paul Nation’s account of meaningful input, output, attention to language and opportunities for fluency practice. A partner can bring back a useful expression naturally later in this conversation or on a future topic. Council of Europe on purposeful language tasks · Paul Nation on the four strands

Build order for Gesprek

  1. Deliver the first exchange. Connect the existing topic field to the lookup. Keep the research brief, source links, fictional role, opinion and rationale in conversation state. Use them in the opening and each subsequent turn. Expose sources beside the conversation and reuse the existing speech and playback controls.
  2. Support both short and long conversations. Let an explicit finish save the exchange first, with optional review and card drafting afterwards. As older turns leave the current 30-line context window, carry forward a short account of what each speaker has said, including why the partner revised an opinion. Retain original learner wording for targeted feedback. If a new factual question needs research, look it up on demand.
  3. Pilot both extremes. End after the opening and after one learner reply. Verify that stopping takes one action and requires no final exercise. Then continue past the history window, revisit an earlier disagreement and change direction. Check whether the partner remembers the learner’s reason, maintains a coherent view, and still offers useful Dutch. Ask learners whether they felt heard and which phrase they would use again. In a later voluntary session, see whether they can use it on another topic.
